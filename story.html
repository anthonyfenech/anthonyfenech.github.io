The Last Game at Tiger Stadium

Fifteen years of memories, one final goodbye, and the stories that never made the headlines

By Anthony Fenech

Detroit Free Press | December 5, 2025

The lights at Comerica Park flickered on for the last time that September evening, casting long shadows across the infield dirt. I had covered hundreds of games by then, but this one felt different. This one felt final. The air carried that peculiar weight of ending, the kind that settles in your chest and reminds you that some moments, once gone, never return in quite the same way.

In the press box, veteran reporters who had seen it all sat in unusual silence. Below us, fans filed in slowly, savoring every moment, knowing that baseball—real baseball, the kind that lives in memory—was about to become history. Some wore jerseys so faded the numbers had nearly disappeared. Others clutched scorecards from decades past, bringing them one final time to the place where those memories were made.

This is the story of that night. But more than that, it's the story of fifteen years spent chronicling a team, a city, and a game that refuses to let go of those who love it most. It's about the conversations that happen in empty clubhouses at midnight, the superstitions that govern a 162-game season, and the humanity that exists in the margins of box scores and headlines.

The press box at Comerica Park during the final game of the 2025 season. Veteran reporters reflected on decades of covering the Tigers, their notebooks filled with stories that would outlive the season itself. (Photo by Staff Photographer)

Miguel Cabrera once told me something I've never forgotten. We were standing near the batting cage on a quiet Tuesday afternoon in April, and he looked out at the empty seats and said, "Every game is somebody's first game. Every game is somebody's last game." At the time, I wrote it down in my notebook and moved on to the next question, the next deadline, the next game. But years later, those words echo differently. They carry weight now.

That's the thing about covering baseball for fifteen years—you accumulate moments like sediment, layer upon layer, until one day you look back and realize you've built something substantial from all those seemingly small interactions. The rookie who couldn't sleep the night before his debut. The veteran taking extra batting practice long after everyone else had gone home. The coach who showed up four hours early every single day for reasons he could never quite articulate.

I remember my first day in the Tigers clubhouse. I was twenty-three years old, fresh out of journalism school, convinced I knew everything about baseball because I'd read every book and studied every statistic. The players could smell my inexperience from across the room. They answered my questions with practiced politeness, the kind reserved for reporters who haven't yet earned the right to real conversation.

• • •

The clubhouse after a loss is a particular kind of quiet. It's not peaceful—it's heavy, thick with disappointment and unspoken questions. After wins, the music plays loud and the laughter echoes off the concrete walls. But after losses, especially the crushing ones, the silence becomes its own language. Players sit at their lockers, staring at nothing in particular, replaying the moment that got away.

On this particular night, even in defeat, there was something else in the air. Relief, maybe. Or gratitude. The strange peace that comes with knowing you gave everything you had, even if it wasn't quite enough. I watched as players cleaned out their lockers, some for the last time. Equipment managers packed away jerseys that would never be worn again. Coaches exchanged handshakes that lasted a beat too long, conveying everything that words couldn't.

There's a ritual to the end of a baseball season. Reporters gather quotes, trying to capture the enormity of finality in sound bites that will be forgotten by morning. Players rush through their routines, eager to begin the long winter of rest and recovery. But occasionally, someone pauses. They take one last look around the room where they spent six months of their lives, and you can see them trying to memorize it, to hold onto something that's already slipping away.

"Every game is somebody's first game. Every game is somebody's last game."

In my fifteen years covering this team, I've learned that the best stories aren't found in post-game quotes or box scores. They're found in the moments between pitches, in the conversations that happen when the cameras are off, in the humanity that exists beneath the uniform. Like the time I found a veteran outfielder teaching a rookie how to tie a tie before a charity event. Or the pitcher who kept a photo of his high school team in his locker, a reminder of where it all began.

Baseball is a game of failure. Even the best hitters fail seven times out of ten. Pitchers give up home runs. Fielders make errors. Managers make decisions that haunt them for years. But what separates the great ones from everyone else isn't talent—it's how they handle those failures. I've seen players spiral after a bad game, convinced they'd lost whatever magic had brought them to the big leagues. And I've seen others treat each failure as information, data to be studied and learned from.

The Tigers of the past fifteen years have embodied both extremes. There were seasons of soaring hope, when October seemed inevitable and the city buzzed with possibility. And there were seasons of grinding disappointment, when every game felt like a referendum on dreams that had long since died. Through it all, the press box remained constant—a place to observe, to document, to try to make sense of the beautiful chaos unfolding below.

Players gathered in the clubhouse following the season finale, reflecting on a year that tested their resilience and resolve. Some would return next season. Others wouldn't. All of them knew this moment wouldn't come again. (Photo by Staff Photographer)

What the fans don't see are the hours before first pitch. The meticulous preparation, the superstitions, the routines that govern a player's day. I've watched infielders take the exact same ground balls in the exact same order for an entire season. Pitchers who eat the same meal before every start. Hitters who arrive at the same time every single day, as if punctuality itself could ward off slumps.

And then there are the injuries—the part of baseball that happens mostly in silence, away from cameras and microphones. The cortisone shots that buy another week. The surgeries that end seasons, sometimes careers. The mental calculations players make every single day: Can I play through this? Should I? What happens if I can't?

I remember interviewing a relief pitcher who'd blown his arm out trying to get that one last strikeout in September. He knew his elbow was compromised. He felt something tear on a fastball two weeks earlier. But he kept pitching because that's what you do—you play until you physically can't anymore. Six months later, he was out of baseball, coaching high school kids in a small town in Texas, his big league dreams reduced to highlights on YouTube.

• • •

Baseball is a game of numbers, but it's also a game of stories. And after fifteen years, I've collected more stories than statistics. Stories about rookies finding their way, veterans hanging on for one more season, and the people behind the scenes who make it all work. The clubhouse attendants who know every player's quirks and preferences. The grounds crew who arrive before dawn to prepare a field that will be torn up by cleats nine hours later. The scouts who spend three hundred days a year in minor league stadiums, searching for the next great player.

There was a scout I met in Toledo who'd been doing this for forty years. He'd discovered three All-Stars and countless solid major leaguers. I asked him once what he looked for in a prospect, expecting some technical answer about swing mechanics or arm angles. Instead, he said, "I watch how they react when they fail. That tells me everything I need to know." He retired two years ago, his eyesight failing, his body worn down by decades of cheap hotels and highway rest stops. But he could still recall every player he'd ever signed, their strengths and weaknesses cataloged in a memory that statistics couldn't capture.

That's what fifteen years teaches you—that baseball is played by humans, covered by humans, and consumed by humans. All those statistics, all those analytics, all those projections and predictions exist to quantify something fundamentally unquantifiable: the human spirit's capacity for both excellence and failure, often within the same at-bat.

As I walked out of the stadium that final night, my phone buzzed with the inevitable question from my editor: "What's next?" But for once, I didn't have an answer ready. After fifteen years of chronicling other people's journeys, I needed to figure out my own. Sometimes you have to let a story end before you can figure out where the next one begins.

The parking lot was nearly empty by the time I reached my car. A few stragglers remained, sitting on their hoods, staring back at the stadium as if looking long enough might change what had just happened. I recognized one of them—a season ticket holder I'd interviewed years ago who'd been coming to games since 1968. He was crying, not ashamed of the tears streaming down his weathered face.

What I do know is this: The game will go on. The season will turn. Winter will give way to spring, and spring training will begin again, full of the usual optimism and possibility that makes baseball baseball. Somewhere, right now, a young reporter is walking into their first press box, notebook in hand, ready to fall in love with this beautiful, maddening, unforgettable game. They'll make the same mistakes I made. They'll ask the obvious questions and miss the subtle stories happening right in front of them.

But if they're lucky, if they stay long enough and pay close enough attention, they'll begin to see what I've seen: that baseball is more than a game. It's a repository for dreams and disappointments, a stage where grown men play a child's game with deadly seriousness, and a daily reminder that failure is not only inevitable but necessary. Because without failure, without loss, without that devastating final out, there can be no triumph. There can be no comeback. There can be no story worth telling.

Just like I did, fifteen years ago, when I walked into that clubhouse for the first time, convinced I knew everything and knowing nothing at all.
